## Biblical studies

Order of work: text → context → interpretation history → current debate.

1. Text: which edition is being used (BHS, NA28, LXX, Vulgate, Peshitta)? Note variants that affect the question.
2. Genre and setting: genre, authorship claims, dating, audience, purpose. State which of these are contested.
3. Exegesis: lexical range in the passage's own language, syntax, structure of the unit, parallels.
4. Redaction/tradition history where relevant: sources, editorial layer, canonical shape.
5. Reception: how major interpretive traditions have read the passage; separate ancient, medieval, modern-critical and confessional readings.
6. Current debate: identify the 2-4 positions that scholars actually argue about today, and what evidence each side uses.

Never present one critical hypothesis as the established result. Note where a claim depends on dating that scholars dispute.

## Patristics and early Christianity

1. Primary text first: critical edition (source, editor, series, date). Quote the edition, not a secondary paraphrase.
2. Transmission: manuscript basis, edition used, translation used and its age and reliability.
3. Context: date, audience, occasion, genre (homily, letter, treatise, polemic), and what the work was arguing against.
4. Doctrinal development: distinguish what the author says from later doctrinal formulations.
5. Reception and canon of the Fathers: which tradition claims this author, and why that matters for the question.
6. Secondary literature: assess whether the scholar works from the primary text or from a translation, and which translation.

## Historical theology

1. Fix the object: doctrine, controversy, council, confession, institution, or person.
2. Primary evidence: conciliar acts, confessional documents, letters, liturgical texts, disciplinary records. Note edition and language.
3. Distinguish the event from its later descriptions; note legendary or polemical layers in the sources.
4. Trace development: earlier formulations, the moment of dispute, the settlement, later reception.
5. Locate the historiography: which school (confessional, Catholic, secular, postcolonial) and what it tends to emphasise.
6. Separate institutional history from doctrinal content when the question mixes them.

## Systematic and constructive theology

1. Identify the tradition and its criteria of authority (scripture, tradition, magisterium, reason, experience) before evaluating an argument.
2. Reconstruct the argument in its strongest form, then test: internal coherence, consistency with its own sources, engagement with the strongest objection.
3. Distinguish a doctrinal claim from a philosophical argument from an empirical claim; they need different evidence.
4. Report the actual positions in the literature rather than a schematic survey.
5. If the user asks for a conclusion within one tradition, apply that tradition's sources and criteria and say so explicitly.

## Practical theology and theology of religious practice

1. Define the practice and its setting (liturgical, devotional, pastoral, communal).
2. Separate normative texts (rubrics, rules, directives) from descriptive evidence (studies, surveys, ethnography, testimony).
3. For empirical claims, check sample, population, method and date; devotional literature is not survey evidence.
4. Note who is speaking: practitioners, formation documents, or outside researchers.

## Comparative and interdisciplinary questions

1. State the comparison criteria explicitly before comparing.
2. Do not treat traditions as internally uniform; name the strand being compared.
3. Do not treat different traditions as directly equivalent without a stated basis.
4. For theology-and-science, theology-and-psychology and similar intersections, keep each discipline's evidence type intact; do not reduce a religious claim to a sociological or psychological explanation unless the question asks for that analysis.

## Choosing the review type

| Situation | Method | What to report |
|---|---|---|
| "What exists on this topic?" | Exploratory/scoping | Search directions, key authors, terminology, coverage limits |
| "How does scholarship argue about this?" | Narrative review | Positions, key works, why they matter, no claim of completeness |
| "Give me a defensible map with criteria" | Structured review | Search strings, dates, inclusion/exclusion, screening, limits |
| Only call it systematic if the procedure was actually followed and documented | | Never claim PRISMA compliance without it |

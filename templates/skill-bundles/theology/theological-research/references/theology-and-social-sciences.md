# Theology and the social sciences

## Name the mode of engagement first

Say explicitly which of these the work is doing, because each has different rules and most errors come from mixing them silently:

| Mode | What it claims | Rule it obeys |
|---|---|---|
| Independence | The two questions are separate and neither judges the other | Do not import conclusions across the boundary |
| Dialogue | Methods and concepts illuminate each other without merging | Each side keeps its own criteria; analogy is not proof |
| Critical correlation | Theological claim and social description are put side by side and each is allowed to question the other | Both sides stay themselves; the correlation is argued, not assumed |
| Integration | One account drawn from both | Highest burden of justification; state how the integration is done |
| Applied/use | Social evidence informs a pastoral decision | The evidence supports a means, not an end |

Practical theology's usual structure for the last mode is Osmer's four tasks: descriptive-empirical (what is going on), interpretive (why), normative (what ought to be), pragmatic (what to do next). Keep the four separate in the write-up; the most common failure is sliding from "what is" to "what ought to be" inside one paragraph.

## Which discipline is competent to say what

| Claim | Competent evidence | Not competent |
|---|---|---|
| "This text says X" | The text, a critical edition, exegesis | A survey, a psychologist's reading |
| "This tradition teaches X" | Confessional documents, magisterial texts, the tradition's own reception | A sociologist's description of adherents |
| "Believers in place P actually do X" | Fieldwork, survey, archive of practice | A theologian's normative text |
| "Practice X is associated with outcome Y" | A study with a stated design | Devotional literature, anecdote |
| "X is true" (theological) | The tradition's sources and arguments | Empirical findings |
| "X is true" (empirical) | Observation and measurement | Doctrinal authority |

Write the level of the claim in the sentence itself, not in a footnote.

## Reading a social-science study as a theologian

Check before citing:

1. **What was measured.** Self-reported belief, attendance, registration in a parish record, or observed practice? These are different facts. In the Czech context, church registration statistics measure registration, not belief or practice.
2. **Who was sampled.** Size, how recruited, which denomination or congregation, which country, which years. A convenience sample from one congregation describes that congregation.
3. **How "religious" was defined.** Tradition, identity, practice, or belief. A study that never defines it cannot support a claim about "believers".
4. **Whose categories are used.** Denominational labels are internally heterogeneous; a Catholic parish, a charismatic community and a Carmelite monastery are not one population.
5. **Design and date.** Cross-sectional data cannot support a claim about change over time. Pre-2020 data may not describe a post-pandemic community.
6. **What the authors themselves claim.** Cite their stated conclusion, not a stronger one you would like.

## Doing research with a religious community

When the community being studied is your own:

- Consent is separate from obedience. A superior's permission is not the participants' consent, and participants must be able to refuse without cost.
- Material from spiritual direction, confession or formation is not research data. Do not use it, and say so in the method section.
- Confidentiality needs a concrete plan: who sees the transcripts, how quotes are anonymised, whether a small community makes a quote identifiable anyway.
- Write a positionality note: your role in the community, how it shaped what people told you, and how it shapes your reading.
- Power asymmetry cuts both ways: state whether you can also be affected by the findings.
- If an institution reviews research ethics, say whether it did; if it did not, state that as a limitation.
- Interview and focus-group questions drafted from a theological conclusion will produce that conclusion. Draft the descriptive questions before forming the normative claim.

## Mixed methods and interdisciplinary reviews

Call a study mixed methods only if the two strands were designed to inform each other and there is a stated point of integration. Two unrelated parts are not mixed methods.

For a review spanning theology and the social sciences:

- Search both literatures. Theological databases (ATLA and similar) and social-science databases index different work; searching only one is a stated limitation, not a coverage claim.
- Write inclusion and exclusion criteria that work across disciplines, including language and source-type rules.
- Keep two evidence tables rather than one merged list, so the type of each claim stays visible.
- Weight is not symmetrical by default: a peer-reviewed study with an appropriate design outranks an opinion piece, and a critical edition outranks a paraphrase, within their own domains.

## Common failure patterns

- A psychology study cited to support a doctrinal or metaphysical claim.
- A national survey used to describe a specific community.
- A theologian cited as evidence that a practice is widespread.
- Social-science consensus presented as settling a question that is not empirical.
- "Dialogue" that is only analogy, with no methodological work done.
- Confirmation-fitting: social evidence selected to illustrate a conclusion already reached.
- Reducing a religious claim to psychology, sociology or politics when the question did not ask for that analysis.
- Treating traditions as internally uniform when comparing them.

## Workflow

1. Split the question into sub-questions and mark each one's type (textual, historical, descriptive, empirical, normative).
2. Assign each sub-question to the discipline whose evidence fits it.
3. Search both literatures; log the searches.
4. Build the two evidence tables.
5. State the integration point explicitly, or state that no integration is attempted.
6. Write with the claim level visible in the sentence; label confidence.
7. In the limitations section, name the disciplines, languages and databases not searched.

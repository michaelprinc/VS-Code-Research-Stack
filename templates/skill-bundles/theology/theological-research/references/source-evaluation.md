## What each source type can establish

| Type | Can establish | Cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Primary source (text, letter, conciliar act, liturgical book, archive record) | What the text says; that a formulation existed at that time and place | What it meant; how representative it was; its causal influence |
| Critical edition / established translation | A checked text and its variants | The translator's interpretive choices are not neutral - name the translation |
| Peer-reviewed article or academic monograph | An interpretation, a reconstruction, an assessment of debate | Correctness - peer review is a quality signal, not a guarantee |
| Dissertation | A detailed treatment, often with a literature survey | Publication status and reception |
| Reference work, encyclopedia, catalog record | Orientation, identification, bibliographic verification | Substantive scholarly argument |
| Church document, magisterial text, official statement | A tradition's official position at a given date | Empirical claims about practice or belief |
| Devotional, spiritual or apologetic writing | A tradition's lived self-understanding | Historical or empirical conclusions |
| News, blog, institutional webpage | Contemporary context, official announcements, primary evidence not published elsewhere | Scholarly assessment |

## Assessment questions

Primary sources: who wrote it, for whom, when, in what genre, what was it arguing against; which edition and translation are being used; how is the text transmitted; what is missing.

Secondary sources: what question the author asks; what method and evidence; does the author engage the strongest opposing view; does the argument rest on primary texts or on other secondary literature; what have later scholars said about it.

Database records: a record proves that a publication exists, not that its content supports the claim. A search-result snippet is not a quotation.

## Access status labels

`metadata_only` · `abstract_available` · `full_text_available` · `partial_text_available` · `access_denied` · `unverified`

These describe access and verification, not quality. A `metadata_only` source may legitimately appear in a bibliography; it must not carry a substantive summary.

## Confidence labels

`[Well-established]` convergent evidence or broad agreement · `[Likely]` credible evidence, some uncertainty · `[Conflicting]` significant evidence or interpretation conflict · `[Limited]` sparse, indirect, inaccessible or methodologically weak evidence.

Explain the basis of the uncertainty when it matters. These are qualitative judgements, not statistics.

## Source-evaluation table

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Reference | Full citation of the work or primary source |
| Source type | Primary / secondary / contextual / tertiary |
| Research focus | Why it is relevant to this question |
| Method | Its analytical approach |
| Contribution | Its argument or evidential value |
| Limitations | Weaknesses and scope restrictions |
| Access status | One of the labels above |
| Verification | Which bibliographic details were checked, and how |
| Relevance | How it enters the synthesis |

Leave a field empty or mark it `unverified`. Never fill a field with a guess.
